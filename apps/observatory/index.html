Millions of lives.
A growing need for better treatments.

Alzheimer’s takes away memory and independence. Its growing human and economic burden makes better treatments urgent. We analyse a research article that offers biological clues to help focus that search.

I’m Ricardo Félix Morais, a neuroradiologist, researcher and MIT Sloan Fellows MBA student. Together with Alex Chen and Seika Karamatsu, I’m examining the findings of a study I coauthored, their importance for treatment research and their wider applications.

The number of people affected is growing.

Prevalence means people living with a condition. In the US, the number aged 65 and older with Alzheimer’s dementia is projected to rise from 7.4 million in 2026 to 12.7 million in 2050, without a breakthrough in prevention or cure. WHO projects 139 million people living with dementia worldwide by 2050.

Incidence means new cases. WHO estimates nearly 10 million new dementia cases worldwide each year. As populations grow and age, more people will need care. Rising totals do not mean that the risk at a given age is increasing everywhere.

Our contribution: make the research and its importance clear.

We analyse what the article found, how strong the evidence is and what it could mean for drug research. We also explain how the approach can be studied in other diseases.

The presentation builds on existing research. It does not claim a new clinical platform, a completed drug experiment or a treatment benefit.

From the scale of Alzheimer’s, to a biological finding, to a clearer direction for treatment research.

What did the article study?

The study I coauthored analysed brain scans from 1,435 participants in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, or ADNI. The study included 201 people with Alzheimer’s, people with milder memory problems, and cognitively normal controls.

Across 82 brain regions, we compared three layers of information:

  1. Where brain tissue was lost.

    MRI scans showed which regions had lower volume in each clinical group compared with controls.

  2. Where chemical receptors are usually found.

    Reference maps from healthy people showed the normal distribution of receptors and transporters involved in communication between brain cells.

  3. Which cellular gene profiles matched those regions.

    We examined reference gene expression patterns associated with different types of brain cells.

The receptor and gene maps came from separate reference datasets. They were not measurements of neurotransmitters or cell activity in these 1,435 participants.

What did the study find?

  1. A serotonin receptor stood out.

    Regions with more of the serotonin receptor 5-HT1A in healthy reference maps tended to overlap with regions showing greater tissue loss. This was the most consistent receptor association across the four clinical groups compared with controls.

  2. Profiles of cells that regulate brain activity also stood out.

    The clearest cellular association involved a gene signature of somatostatin, or SST, inhibitory neurons. These neurons help control the activity of other neurons. Signatures of another inhibitory group, called VIP, also showed associations, although less consistently.

  3. The findings also related to cognition.

    Some scores derived from these patterns were associated with performance on cognitive tests. The broadest set of associations was seen in the group with late mild cognitive impairment. This was a comparison at one point in time, not a prediction of future decline.

We found that the geography of brain tissue loss aligns with specific chemical and cellular features. This gives researchers a more focused biological question to test.

Source: the 2026 manuscript described in Our scientific foundation. The strongest receptor and SST pattern associations were observed in late mild cognitive impairment; the greatest tissue loss occurred in Alzheimer’s dementia.

Why could this matter for medicines?

A drug needs a biological target: something it can change to produce a useful effect. Our findings point towards serotonin receptor biology and inhibitory cell function as questions worth investigating in Alzheimer’s.

Medicines already exist that bind to 5-HT1A receptors. This makes the receptor a practical starting point for laboratory research, including the possibility of studying existing compounds for a new use.

The next question is whether changing this signalling can help preserve useful brain function. Our study did not test a drug, establish whether increasing or decreasing receptor activity would help, or show a treatment benefit.

The opportunity is to use a biological clue from human brain imaging to design a better treatment experiment.

Existing receptor pharmacology: buspirone prescribing information describes binding to 5-HT1A and an anxiety indication. It does not establish an Alzheimer’s treatment effect.

Could we use this approach in other diseases?

Yes. The method compares a disease pattern with biological reference maps, so it can be applied to other conditions with suitable imaging data. The result must be tested separately in each disease.

Our earlier published work already used MRI and neurotransmitter maps in Alzheimer’s, behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia and mild cognitive impairment. Different conditions showed different associations.

The newer study adds cellular gene profiles. Applying that full combination to another disease is a next research step. We cannot assume that the same receptor or cell profile will matter everywhere.
