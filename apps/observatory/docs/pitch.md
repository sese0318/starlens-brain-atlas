# DopaTeam: five minute pitch

This is the spoken pitch published on [our website](https://dopateam.vercel.app/impact.html#pitch). Sources and qualifications for the impact figures are linked on that page.

A five minute pitch · about 600 words

Why this finding deserves attention.

Alzheimer’s changes the meaning of ordinary life. A familiar route becomes confusing. A conversation disappears from memory. A person who has always been independent begins to need help with the simplest things. Their family’s life changes too.

In the United States, an estimated 7.4 million people aged 65 and older are living with Alzheimer’s dementia. That number is projected to reach 12.7 million by 2050, without a breakthrough in prevention or cure. Worldwide, 57 million people were living with dementia in 2021, and Alzheimer’s accounts for most cases. WHO projects 139 million people with dementia by 2050.

Nearly 10 million new dementia cases occur worldwide each year. This growing need is driven in part by populations living longer. Whatever happens to individual risk, the number of people needing help makes the search for better treatments urgent.

The economic consequences are enormous. US health and long term care spending for people with Alzheimer’s and other dementias is projected at 409 billion dollars in 2026, excluding unpaid care. The worldwide economic cost of dementia was already 1.3 trillion dollars in 2019, including informal care. These figures use different years and definitions, but both show the scale of the problem.

The medicines market is much smaller, but substantial. Commercial estimates for 2025 range from about 1,900 to 2,300 million dollars in the US and 4,700 to 6,500 million worldwide. These are estimates of the treatment market, not the full cost of the disease. Better treatments could matter to patients, families and health systems as well as to medicine developers.

I’m Ricardo Félix Morais, a neuroradiologist, researcher and MIT Sloan Fellows MBA student. With Alex Chen and Seika Karamatsu, I’m analysing a research article I coauthored and explaining why its findings deserve attention.

The study began with a question: why are some brain regions more affected than others? We analysed scans from 1,435 participants, including people with Alzheimer’s, milder memory problems and cognitively normal controls. We compared tissue loss across 82 brain regions with reference maps of chemical receptors and patterns of gene activity associated with different brain cells.

Two features stood out. One was a serotonin receptor called 5-HT1A. The other was the gene profile of inhibitory neurons, especially a group called SST. These cells help regulate the activity of other neurons. The brain regions showing greater tissue loss tended to overlap with these reference patterns.

Why could that help treatment research? A medicine needs something biological to act on. Our findings give researchers more specific systems to investigate. There are already compounds that bind to this serotonin receptor. What remains unknown is whether changing that system can preserve useful function in Alzheimer’s, and under what conditions.

My earlier research is published. The study we analyse here is a 2026 research manuscript. It did not test a drug or establish a treatment benefit. Our presentation examines the evidence, its limits and the questions it opens.

The broader approach also applies beyond Alzheimer’s. My published work includes frontotemporal dementia. The method can be studied in other diseases, but each needs its own evidence. The newer combination with cellular gene profiles also needs separate validation.

Our contribution is a clear analysis of that research and its potential. We want people to understand what the study found and why it could help focus future treatment studies. The reason to pursue those studies is human: preserving memory, independence and more time together.
